## The problem

A Claude plan has two limits: a five-hour session window and a weekly cap. Both are visible, but only on a settings page in claude.ai. So you find out you are close to a limit when you are already there, usually halfway through a task.

The question I wanted answered wasn't _"what percentage am I at?"_ It was _"can I keep going?"_ That is a different question, and it leads to a different design.

## Designed for a glance

The menu bar title is the product. Everything else is detail. It reads like this:

Session usage, weekly usage, and the time until the session resets. The face in front does the real work: you don't read it, you notice it. A rocket means go. A skull means wait for the reset. For people who find that too playful there is a monochrome ring that fills up and only takes on colour from 75%.

Keeping that one line accurate turned out to matter more than anything else. At one point I let macOS throttle the app to save energy, and refreshes stretched from five minutes to nine. I reversed that. An energy saving that makes the menu bar wrong defeats the point of the app.

## A percentage needs a clock

The weekly limit showed the same problem in a smaller form. _41% used_ tells you very little on its own. On day two of the week that is a lot. On day six it is nothing.

So the weekly bar has two fills: a light one for how much of the week has passed, and the usage in front of it. If usage runs ahead of time, you are spending faster than the week allows. The number became a rate instead of a level, without adding a chart, an extra screen or a single new word to learn.

## Interrupt only when it matters

Notifications come at 80% and 95% of the session and at 90% of the week. Each warning is sent once per window, so you are never told the same thing twice. When a limit resets, the app only says so if it warned you before. A reset you didn't know you were waiting for is noise.

Turning notifications off still keeps track of which thresholds have passed. Otherwise, switching them back on would replay every warning for the current window at once.

## Small things that make it feel calm

  light={settingsLight}

- **Nothing moves under your cursor.** The popover is anchored to the menu bar title. A shorter title would make it jump, so the title doesn't change while the popover is open.
- **Settings stay out of the way.** The main view shows usage and nothing else. A connection problem, a Claude outage or an available update shows up as a dot on the gear.
- **Colour that is also readable.** The session percentage takes the green-to-red stress colour, on a card tinted with that same colour. That failed contrast. The text colour is now stepped towards black or white until it passes WCAG AA, keeping its hue. I checked it at every percentage from 0 to 100, in both themes.
- **Don't guess.** If the app doesn't recognise a plan, it shows no plan name instead of a likely one. Unusual account states are logged first, so there are real examples to design for before any UI exists.
- **Nothing changes silently.** New installs get the ring icon. Existing users keep the emoji they already had.

## Trust is a feature

The app reads usage the same way claude.ai does, with the session of the Claude desktop app that is already signed in. Nothing goes to anyone else. Usage history stays on your Mac. Updates are signed, and the app rejects any update without a valid signature. Uninstalling is a button in settings, and you choose whether to keep your history.

None of this shows on screen. It is still the part I would defend first.

## Rewriting it without changing it

Version 1 was a Python app. Version 2 is a native Swift rewrite, and from the outside it should look identical. To make sure, the Python version stayed in the repository as the reference: the same inputs go into both, and the Swift version has to produce exactly the same output, down to how a colour is rounded.

The rewrite was also a chance to make it lighter. The first version loaded claude.ai in a hidden browser to fetch the numbers, peaking at around 400 MB of memory. Version 2 asks for the same data directly and only falls back to the browser when it has to. Existing users moved to version 2 through the normal in-app update, with their settings and history intact.

## How it was made

I built Claude Stats with Claude Code, in four focused days spread over two months. The code was largely written by Claude. What it should do, what it shouldn't do, and when something was good enough were my decisions. Most of the work in this case study is exactly that: choosing a face over a number, a clock next to a percentage, silence over another notification.

That is the work I wrote about in [Judgement as a learnable skill](/notes/judgement-as-a-learnable-skill). This project is what it looks like in practice.

## Try it

Claude Stats runs on macOS 11 or later and uses the account that is signed in to the Claude desktop app. [Download the latest version](https://github.com/josbez/claude-usage/releases/latest/download/ClaudeUsage.dmg) and follow the install steps in the [README](https://github.com/josbez/claude-usage#install-dmg).
